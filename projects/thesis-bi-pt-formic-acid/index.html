At a glance

Problem
Platinum oxidises formic acid well at first, but adsorbed CO builds up and the current falls within an hour.
My contribution
Made and characterised four Bi-modified Pt/C catalysts, tuned the catalyst ink, and tested them with and without a quinone mediator (AQSA) in a three-electrode half-cell.
Key result
Bi(1.25)-Pt/C, with the least bismuth, retained 92.9% of its current over a one-hour hold, against 58.5% for commercial Pt/C. The mediator did not improve retention.
Limits
Half-cell, acid, one hour. No replicate counts or error bars are reported, and there is no membrane electrode assembly or full-cell test yet.

Problem and baseline

Formic acid is an attractive fuel for small fuel cells: it is liquid, easy to store, and crosses a Nafion membrane far less than methanol does. Platinum oxidises it well at first, but a side reaction leaves carbon monoxide stuck to the surface. As the platinum fills up, the current drops within the first hour.

Baseline. Commercial Pt/C is the reference throughout. In the one-hour hold it retained 58.5% of its current.

I tested two ways of protecting the platinum, and whether they help or hinder each other: decorating the Pt/C with bismuth, which is known to block the CO-forming path, and adding a dissolved quinone, anthraquinone-2-sulfonic acid (AQSA), as a redox mediator in the electrolyte.

My contribution

This was my M.Tech thesis, supervised by Prof. Raghuram Chetty at IIT Madras, with the catalyst synthesis done in the KinCat group at NTNU. I made the four catalysts, formulated the inks and prepared the electrodes, ran the voltammetry and the one-hour holds on an Autolab potentiostat, and analysed the results, using FE-SEM, EDX, XRD and BET data to connect bismuth loading with morphology and activity.

Methods and assumptions

  1. Made a series of catalysts

    Commercial Pt/C was modified with bismuth at four loadings by a wet-chemical route: adsorption from solution under an inert atmosphere, chemical reduction and a thermal treatment.

  2. Tracked where the bismuth went

    FE-SEM with EDX, powder XRD and, for the best catalyst, nitrogen physisorption (BET).

  3. Tuned the catalyst ink

    Compared two Nafion binder contents in a water and isopropanol ink, and kept the thinner film for the later comparisons.

  4. Built and tested electrodes

    Drop-cast electrodes on a glassy carbon disk for voltammetry, the electrode in the photos, and on carbon paper for the separate one-hour holds, in a three-electrode cell with a platinum counter and an Ag/AgCl reference. The quinone mediator was screened at several concentrations.

  5. Ran durability holds

    Each catalyst, on carbon paper, was held at a fixed potential for one hour, with and without the mediator.

Electrolyte
Formic acid in dilute sulphuric acid, nitrogen-purged, room temperature
Retention
Current at the end of the hour as a share of the current just after the first-minute charging transient.
Mass activity
Peak current per mass of platinum on the electrode.
Potentials
Measured against Ag/AgCl and reported on the RHE scale.

Results

Where the bismuth went

At the two lowest loadings there were no separate bismuth crystals in SEM or XRD: bismuth sat on the platinum as a thin decoration. At the highest loading, bright Bi-rich clusters appeared and covered much of the platinum.

Less Nafion, more current

On the glassy carbon electrode, the thinner Nafion film gave roughly 2.5–2.8 times the peak current of the thicker one, even though the thicker film showed slightly more active area on plain Pt/C. The active-area measurement only needs protons to reach the platinum, but formic acid has to diffuse through the film, so a thick ionomer layer wins one test and loses the other. The effect was found on Pt/C and then seen again on a Bi-modified catalyst.

Activity is not durability

A mid-range bismuth loading gave the most impressive voltammogram, but most of its peak came from oxidising CO that had already formed: it was recovering from poisoning rather than avoiding it, and it decayed fastest of the modified catalysts in the one-hour hold. The lowest-bismuth catalyst gave a much higher current on the direct pathway, before any CO is burned off, than commercial Pt/C.

Which catalyst lasted

  • The least bismuth worked best for stability. Bi(1.25)-Pt/C, the lowest loading, retained the most current in both electrolytes.
  • Retention is not output. Retention measures decay, not current. After an hour, a higher-loading catalyst still delivered more current because it started much higher; the lowest-bismuth catalyst’s advantage is that it decayed far less, ending only slightly above commercial Pt/C.
  • The mediator did not help in these tests. At the concentration used for the comparison, AQSA lowered peak activity and one-hour retention on the catalysts compared.

Limitations

  • Half-cell, acid, one hour. Everything was measured in a three-electrode half-cell at room temperature. The numbers rank catalysts against each other; they are not fuel-cell performance. Practical fuel cells run for hundreds to thousands of hours, and the thesis names a membrane electrode assembly with Bi(1.25)-Pt/C, in situ spectroscopy, and longer degradation tests as the next steps.
  • Repeatability is not reported. The thesis gives no replicate counts, standard deviations or error bars for the voltammetry or the durability holds, so differences should be read as indicative rankings, not statistically tested results. Two trends were seen again on a second catalyst, which is a check on the trend, not a replicate.
  • Coarse bismuth grid. Four bismuth loadings were tested, and the best was the lowest. The true optimum may sit between or below them.
  • Mechanism is inferred. Third-body blocking of CO-forming sites is the explanation that fits the data; it was not observed directly. The schematic above is illustrative.

What I took from it. A good-looking voltammogram is not the same as a durable catalyst, and retention is not the same as output. I now check durability before ranking anything, and I think about what each measurement can and cannot see.
