At a glance

Problem
Platinum oxidises formic acid well at first, but adsorbed CO builds up and the current falls within an hour.
My contribution
Made and characterised four Bi-modified Pt/C catalysts, tuned the catalyst ink, and tested them with and without a quinone mediator (AQSA) in a three-electrode half-cell.
Key result
Bi(1.25)-Pt/C, with the least bismuth, retained 92.9% of its current over a one-hour hold, against 58.5% for commercial Pt/C. The mediator did not improve retention.
Limits
Half-cell, acid, one hour. No replicate counts or error bars are reported, and there is no membrane electrode assembly or full-cell test yet.

Problem and baseline

Formic acid is an attractive fuel for small fuel cells: it is liquid, easy to store, and crosses a Nafion membrane far less than methanol does. Platinum oxidises it well at first, but a side reaction leaves carbon monoxide stuck to the surface. As the platinum fills up, the current drops within the first hour.

Baseline. Commercial 20 wt% Pt/C is the reference throughout. In the one-hour hold it retained 58.5% of its current, and in the standard voltammetry its peak mass activity was 505 mA per mg of Pt.

I tested two ways of protecting the platinum, and whether they help or hinder each other: decorating the Pt/C with bismuth, which is known to block the CO-forming path, and adding a dissolved quinone, anthraquinone-2-sulfonic acid (AQSA), as a redox mediator in the electrolyte.

My contribution

This was my M.Tech thesis, supervised by Prof. Raghuram Chetty at IIT Madras, with the catalyst synthesis done in the KinCat group at NTNU. I made the four catalysts, formulated the inks and prepared the electrodes, ran the voltammetry and the one-hour holds on an Autolab potentiostat, and analysed the results, using FE-SEM, EDX, XRD and BET data to connect bismuth loading with morphology, lattice strain and activity.

Methods and assumptions

  1. Made four catalysts

    Commercial 20 wt% Pt/C (60 mg) was soaked for 48 hours in bismuth nitrate at 1.25, 2.5, 5 and 10 mM in 20 mL of dilute sulphuric acid under nitrogen, then reduced with sodium borohydride and annealed at 300 °C for 2 h under argon.

  2. Tracked where the bismuth went

    FE-SEM, EDX averaged over four spots per sample, powder XRD and, for the best catalyst, nitrogen physisorption (BET).

  3. Tuned the catalyst ink

    5 mg of catalyst in water and isopropanol, with 5 µL or 20 µL of 5 wt% Nafion as binder.

  4. Built and tested electrodes

    For voltammetry, 4 µL of ink was drop-cast on a polished glassy carbon disk (0.0707 cm²), the electrode in the photos. For the separate one-hour holds, the same ink was applied to 1 cm² carbon paper at the same loading, for a larger, more even area. Both were measured in a three-electrode cell with a platinum-coil counter and an Ag/AgCl reference. AQSA was screened at 0, 1, 2 and 10 mM.

  5. Ran durability holds

    Each catalyst, on carbon paper, was held at 0.55 V vs RHE for 3600 s, with and without 1 mM AQSA.

Electrolyte
0.5 M formic acid in 0.5 M H2SO4, nitrogen-purged, room temperature (22 ± 2 °C)
Retention
Current at 3600 s divided by current at 61 s. The first 60 s are discarded as the charging transient.
Mass activity
Peak current divided by the platinum mass on the electrode, taken from each catalyst’s EDX platinum fraction.
Current density
Per geometric area: 0.0707 cm² for the glassy carbon disk, 1 cm² for carbon paper.
Potentials
Measured against Ag/AgCl and converted to the RHE scale.

Results

Where the bismuth went

At the two lowest loadings there were no separate bismuth crystals in SEM or XRD: bismuth sat on the platinum as a thin decoration. At 10 mM, bright Bi-rich clusters appeared and covered much of the platinum.

Less Nafion, more current

On the glassy carbon electrode, the thinner Nafion film gave higher peak current than the thicker one, even though the thicker film showed slightly more active area on plain Pt/C. The active-area measurement only needs protons to reach the platinum, but formic acid has to diffuse through the film, so a thick ionomer layer wins one test and loses the other. The effect was found on Pt/C and then reproduced on Bi(2.5)-Pt/C.

Bi(5)-Pt/C had the most impressive voltammogram, 852 mA per mg of Pt, but most of that peak came from oxidising CO that had already formed. It was recovering from poisoning rather than avoiding it, and it kept only 52.8% of its current in the one-hour hold. On the direct pathway, before any CO is burned off, Bi(1.25)-Pt/C reached 48.2 mA/cm², about 4.8 times commercial Pt/C (10.1 mA/cm²).

  • The least bismuth worked best for stability. Bi(1.25)-Pt/C retained the most current in both electrolytes and had the highest peak mass activity with AQSA (497 mA per mg of Pt).
  • Retention is not output. After an hour in formic acid, Bi(5)-Pt/C still delivered the highest current (12.3 mA/cm²), because it started from a much higher level and decayed more. Bi(1.25)-Pt/C’s advantage is that it decayed far less: its current at one hour (9.7 mA/cm²) is only slightly above that of commercial Pt/C (9.3).
  • The mediator did not help in these tests. At 1 mM, AQSA lowered peak mass activity on every catalyst compared and lowered one-hour retention on all five. In an earlier screen on Pt/C with the thicker ink, 1 mM raised the peak slightly (219 vs 188 mA per mg of Pt) and 2 and 10 mM suppressed it. On Bi(5)-Pt/C, 1 mM was again the least suppressive concentration tested, though it still cut the peak by 44%.

Limitations

  • Half-cell, acid, one hour. Everything was measured in a three-electrode half-cell at room temperature. The numbers rank catalysts against each other; they are not fuel-cell performance. Practical fuel cells run for hundreds to thousands of hours, and the thesis names a membrane electrode assembly with Bi(1.25)-Pt/C, in situ spectroscopy, and longer degradation tests as the next steps.
  • Repeatability is not reported. The thesis gives no replicate counts, standard deviations or error bars for the voltammetry or the durability holds, so differences should be read as indicative rankings, not statistically tested results. The only spreads reported are the four-spot EDX average and a ±1.05 m²/g fit uncertainty on the BET area of one catalyst. Two trends were reproduced on a second catalyst (the Nafion effect on Bi(2.5)-Pt/C and the AQSA trend on Bi(5)-Pt/C), which is a check on the trend, not a replicate.
  • Coarse bismuth grid. Four loadings from 1.25 to 10 mM were tested, and the best was the lowest. The true optimum may sit between or below them.
  • Mechanism is inferred. Third-body blocking of CO-forming sites is the explanation that fits the data; it was not observed directly. The schematic above is illustrative.

What I took from it. A good-looking voltammogram is not the same as a durable catalyst, and retention is not the same as output. I now check durability before ranking anything, and I think about what each measurement can and cannot see.
