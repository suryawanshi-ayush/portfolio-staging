At a glance

Problem
Platinum oxidises formic acid well at first, but adsorbed CO builds up and the current falls within an hour.
My contribution
Made and characterised four Bi-modified Pt/C catalysts, tuned the catalyst ink, and tested them with and without a quinone mediator (AQSA) in a three-electrode half-cell.
Key result
Bi(1.25)-Pt/C, with the least bismuth, retained 92.9% of its current over a one-hour hold, against 58.5% for commercial Pt/C. The mediator did not improve retention.
Limits
Half-cell, acid, one hour. No replicate counts or error bars are reported, and there is no membrane electrode assembly or full-cell test yet.

The question

Formic acid is an attractive fuel for small fuel cells: it is a liquid, easy to store, and crosses the membrane far less than methanol does. Platinum oxidises it well at first, but a side reaction leaves carbon monoxide (CO) stuck to the surface, and the current falls within the first hour.

The thesis asked two things: can a small amount of bismuth on commercial Pt/C stop the CO from forming, and does a dissolved quinone mediator (anthraquinone-2-sulfonic acid, AQSA) help further? The reference throughout was the same commercial Pt/C, unmodified.

My contribution and approach

This was my M.Tech thesis at IIT Madras, supervised by Prof. Raghuram Chetty, with the catalyst synthesis carried out during my research stay at NTNU. I did the experimental work and the analysis:

  • Made a series of catalysts: commercial Pt/C modified with bismuth at four loadings by a wet-chemical route.
  • Checked where the bismuth went with electron microscopy and element mapping (FE-SEM, EDX), X-ray diffraction and, for the best catalyst, surface-area measurement.
  • Built and tested electrodes in a three-electrode half-cell on an Autolab potentiostat: cyclic voltammetry for activity, and one-hour constant-potential holds for durability, with and without the mediator.
  • Tuned the catalyst ink, comparing two ionomer (Nafion) contents before the main comparisons.

Key findings

1. The least bismuth lasted longest

  • Bi(1.25)-Pt/C92.9%
  • Bi(2.5)-Pt/C67.4%
  • Commercial Pt/C58.5%
  • Bi(5)-Pt/C52.8%
  • Bi(10)-Pt/C39.3%

The catalyst with the least bismuth, Bi(1.25)-Pt/C, kept 92.9% of its current over the hour, against 58.5% for commercial Pt/C. More bismuth did not help: the highest loading was the least stable of all five (39.3%).

2. A strong voltammogram is not a durable catalyst

A mid-range loading, Bi(5)-Pt/C, gave the highest voltammetric peak of the series, but most of that peak came from burning off CO that had already formed: it was recovering from poisoning, not avoiding it. In the one-hour hold it kept only 52.8%, the second-lowest. Retention is not output either: because it started higher, Bi(5)-Pt/C still delivered the most current at the end of the hour, while Bi(1.25)-Pt/C ended only slightly above commercial Pt/C. Its advantage is that it decayed far less.

3. The mediator did not help in these tests

Adding AQSA lowered one-hour retention on all five catalysts; for the best catalyst it fell from 92.9% to 70.4%, and for commercial Pt/C from 58.5% to 55.6%. Bi(1.25)-Pt/C stayed the most durable with the mediator too.

4. Less ionomer, more current

A thinner Nafion film in the catalyst ink gave roughly 2.5–2.8 times the forward-scan peak current of a thicker one, on both commercial Pt/C and a Bi-modified catalyst. On plain Pt/C the thicker film even showed more active platinum area, yet less current: measuring active area only needs protons to reach the platinum, while formic acid has to diffuse through the film. The thinner ink was used for all later comparisons.

5. Where the bismuth sat

At the two lowest loadings, microscopy and X-ray diffraction showed no separate bismuth crystals, consistent with a thin decoration on the platinum. At the highest loading, bismuth-rich clusters appeared and covered much of the platinum, which is consistent with its poor stability.

What this does not show

  • Not fuel-cell performance. Everything was measured in a three-electrode half-cell in acid at room temperature, over one hour. The results rank catalysts against each other. Practical fuel cells run for hundreds to thousands of hours; a membrane electrode assembly and single-cell tests are the next step named in the thesis.
  • No repeatability data. The thesis reports no replicate counts, standard deviations or error bars, so the differences are indicative rankings, not statistically tested ones.
  • A coarse loading grid. Four bismuth loadings were tested and the best was the lowest, so the true optimum may lie lower still.
  • The mechanism is inferred. Bismuth blocking the sites where CO forms is the explanation that fits the data; it was not observed directly, and the illustration above is conceptual.

What I took from it. A good-looking voltammogram is not the same as a durable catalyst, and retention is not the same as output. I now check durability before ranking anything, and I ask what each measurement can and cannot see.
